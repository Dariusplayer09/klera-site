Most tutors arrive too late.

Klera watches you solve maths on an iPad and writes help back onto the board, in handwriting.

See how it reads the pen Try the five actions

Move your pointer over the board. Click it to write the line again.

By the time a student says they are stuck, the struggle has already happened.

The pause before the wrong line, the pressure in the pen, the line rewritten three times. A tutor who only hears “I’m stuck” missed all of it. Klera reads the work while it is happening.

Write anywhere. Nothing is a text box.

The canvas is infinite and free-form. Klera finds the problems on its own: it groups strokes into clusters, one per problem, and follows each one as its own attempt. Write six problems down the page and it is tracking six pieces of work.

Five ways to ask. Only one gives the answer.

The usual failure of an AI tutor is that every button quietly turns into “tell me the answer”. Klera’s five actions have strict scopes that are enforced separately. Open one to see what it is allowed to do.

It writes. It does not type.

When Klera answers on the board, the answer is drawn stroke by stroke. The target is a calm, deliberate tutor’s hand: not shaky, not decorative, and never visibly generated.

An expression is a set of strokes

Stroke order, pen lifts and the rhythm between glyphs are what make a hand read as a hand. A picture of handwriting shows none of that.

Structure before strokes

The newest engine parses an expression into structure first, so a fraction knows it is a fraction and an exponent knows it is raised. Then it lays out glyphs, composes pen strokes, and times them.

Never a blank board

Three generations of the engine coexist on purpose. If any stage of the newest one fails, rendering falls back to an older glyph-stamp engine instead of failing in front of a student.

When a picture is the explanation.

Some ideas are not sentences. For those, Klera offers a dynamic visualisation, and only when the concept genuinely has one. The model is told to leave the suggestion out when a picture would add nothing.

Every showing is measured afterwards: did the student solve the problem, ask for a hint instead, or close it within seconds? That makes it possible to know which visualisations teach anything.

Read about the learner profile

Hint

What it does
Gives exactly one nudge, phrased as a question or a pointer. Enough to get moving again, never enough to finish.
What it will not do
Reveal the answer, show the full solution, or write anything on the board.
What reaches the board
Nothing. The board stays yours until you earn the next line.

Mistake

What it does
Finds where the reasoning first broke and names the strategy for correcting it.
What it will not do
Solve the rest of the problem or continue past the error.
What reaches the board
Nothing. It tells you where to look, not what to write.

Next step

What it does
Gives exactly one step, continuing from the work already on the board.
What it will not do
Restate what you already wrote, hand the original problem back, or work toward the final answer.
What reaches the board
One new line in handwriting below your work: the actual result of taking that single step.

Explain

What it does
Teaches the concept: what kind of problem this is, the key principle, and why the method works. Ends with a question to check understanding.
What it will not do
Solve this problem, walk through its particular steps, or give the final answer.
What reaches the board
Nothing. Explain is about understanding, so it leaves the board untouched.

Solve

What it does
Finishes the problem, lists every solution, and checks the result.
What it will not do
Nothing is off limits here. It is the one action allowed to complete the work, and it is recorded as reliance.
What reaches the board
The full worked solution, written line by line. Using it counts against independence, which the learner profile reads.
