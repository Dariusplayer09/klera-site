01 — The idea

Most tutors arrive too late.

By the time a student asks for help, the struggle already happened. The pause before the wrong line, the pressure in the pen, the line rewritten three times — all of it is gone by the time anyone says "I'm stuck".

Klera is an iPad whiteboard that watches the work as it happens. You write maths with an Apple Pencil the way you always would. It reads the ink, judges the working, and when it helps, it writes back onto the board in handwriting — not a chat bubble, not a wall of text. A line of maths, in the place the line belongs.

02 — The board

Write anywhere. Nothing is a text box.

The canvas is infinite and free-form. Klera finds the problems by itself — it groups strokes into clusters, one per problem, and tracks each one as a separate piece of work with its own history. Write six problems down the page and it is following six independent attempts.

03 — The five actions

Five ways to ask. Each one refuses to do the others' job.

The failure mode of an AI tutor is that every button quietly becomes "give me the answer". Klera's five actions have strict, separately enforced scopes. Explain is forbidden from solving the problem. Next Step is forbidden from working toward the final answer. Only Solve finishes anything.

04 — The handwriting engine

It writes. It does not type.

When Klera answers on the board, the answer is drawn stroke by stroke, in a hand. This is the part of the app that took the longest and is the easiest to get wrong: the target is a calm, deliberate, premium tutor's hand — not shaky, not artistic, not over-humanised, and above all not visibly generated.

05 — Visualisation

When a picture is the explanation.

Some ideas are not sentences. For those, Klera offers a dynamic visualisation — and only when the concept genuinely has one. The model is explicitly instructed to omit the suggestion when a picture would add nothing, which is why you do not get a decorative diagram on every problem.

Every showing is also measured: whether the student then solved the problem, asked for a hint instead, or dismissed it in two seconds. That is a before-and-after on whether the picture actually taught anything — which feeds the learner profile.

Offered, not imposed

A visualisation is a one-line invitation, never an interruption. Decline it and nothing changes.

Concept-gated

No picture is generated unless the underlying idea has a genuinely useful dynamic form.

Scored afterwards

Solved-after, hint-after and closed-quickly are recorded per template, so effectiveness is measurable rather than assumed.

06 — Next

The part that is actually hard.

Everything above is the surface. The thing underneath — the reason Klera can tell the difference between a student who is thinking and a student who is stuck — is the learner profile. It is built per student, from their own pen, and it does not work the way you would guess.
