Five principles

Every design decision on this page traces back to one of these.

01

Ink, not text

The tutor's answer must be erasable, selectable ink on the student's own canvas, in the same coordinate space. A rendered string or a video does not belong to the page.

02

The individual, not the population

Every signal is read against the student's own normal. A threshold that works on the average student is wrong for most real ones.

03

Fusion, not a detector

No single signal is allowed to decide anything. Each is weak and noisy on its own; accuracy comes from the combination.

04

Withhold by default

The hardest thing in tutoring is not answering. When uncertain, show; when confident a student is ready, make them do it.

05

Refuse rather than mislead

A confidently wrong diagram or a wrong verdict teaches something false. Every generative step can be refused, and the floor cannot fail.

Handwriting

Why generated maths looks generated, and what to do about it.

Human ink, arranged by a typesetter

Synthesising strokes from a formula produces handwriting that is too regular to read as human. Klera instead draws from captured human strokes and places them into a mathematical layout, so the geometry is correct and the line quality is real. Common multi-symbol runs are captured as whole phrases, because their internal spacing is exactly what symbol-by-symbol drawing gets wrong.

Correlated variation, not jitter

The tell of synthetic handwriting is independent randomness: every glyph wobbles on its own. A real hand varies coherently. Slant, size and baseline drift are properties of the writer on that day, and they change slowly across a line. So the variation is decided once per piece of writing and applied smoothly, with only a small, bounded amount left independent per symbol.

Degrade gracefully, never visibly

Coverage is never complete. When a symbol has no captured template, older rendering generations step in for that symbol, that line, or the whole render, in that order. Every level is silent, and none of them can emit garbage ink. Keeping the old engines is not nostalgia; it is the fallback.

Deterministic by design

The same input renders identically twice. Timing is derived from the strokes themselves, and one schedule is the single source of truth for the animation, the debug overlay and the tests. That turns “does this look right?” from a human judging screenshots into an assertion.

Written at reading pace

Strokes appear at the speed of a calm, deliberate tutor, so a student can follow the line being formed. Instant output is faster and teaches less.

The visual engine

Generating a diagram is easy. Refusing a wrong one is the work.

The model recommends, the engine decides

A language model is good at noticing that a picture would help and bad at knowing when its picture is wrong. So its role is advisory. Deterministic rules decide what kind of visual a problem actually earns, for example refusing a third dimension unless the maths genuinely lives in space.

A cascade, from most trusted to least

Hand-built, validated templates come first. Generated visuals are tried only when no template fits, and each one must pass a gate: a static check on what it is allowed to do, and a behavioural check that it actually renders something meaningful. A refusal at any level falls through to the next.

A floor that cannot fail

The last level is local and deterministic, and it never invents mathematics. If nothing honest can be drawn for this problem, it says so and offers to try again. Everything runs on the device, so there is no network between a student and their diagram.

The learner profile

A model of one student, built from evidence a transcript cannot contain.

A chat tutor sees a student twice per exchange. Everything a human tutor reacts to happens in between. The profile exists to capture that gap, and to be honest about how much it actually knows.

Six families of evidence

Each family answers a question a transcript-based tutor cannot.

Pen behaviourHow the pen moves: hesitation, fluency, pressure, erasingThe long stare at a blank page never reaches a chat window.

From raw signal to a teaching decision

Measure

Every family is observed on every attempt, continuously and invisibly.

Normalise

Each signal becomes a deviation from this student's own baseline, never a population average. Nothing is scored until the baseline has seen enough clean work.

Interpret

Deviations are combined into states, each carrying its own confidence. An attempt with four agreeing signals moves the model further than one with a single signal.

Remember

Slow traits accumulate across sessions and deliberately decay. One bad afternoon cannot rewrite a student, and last term's verdict cannot outlive the student it described.

Act, then check

The profile chooses how to teach, and every choice is scored by what the student did next.

Confusion and frustration need opposite responses

Most affect models collapse everything unpleasant into one “struggling” bucket. Learning research says that is a mistake. We follow the flow model: challenge and skill have to stay in balance, and the states either side of that balance call for different moves.

Boredom

Skill has outrun the challenge. The answer is to make it harder. Helping more makes it worse.

Confusion

Stuck but still attempting. This is where learning happens. The smallest possible nudge, then leave them alone.

Frustration

Confusion escalating toward giving up. Rescue early; the read is deliberately biased toward catching it.

Why per-student, and why fusion

The obvious design is a global threshold: this much pressure means stress. Instrumented sessions on real iPads showed why that fails. For one student, being stuck shows up as pressing harder. For another, pressure is completely flat, and being stuck shows up as going still before the pen touches the page. The channel that separates one student is flat for the other.

Help is a measurement, not just a menu

A solve after a small hint is strong evidence of skill; a solve after the full answer is almost none. Weighting evidence by the help that preceded it means a student cannot inflate their own model by asking for solutions.

The loop that closes

Each way of teaching is treated as an option to be evaluated per student, and scored by what the student did next. Over time the profile models not just the student, but how that student responds to being taught a particular way.

Absence of evidence is not evidence

“We have not checked this yet” must never be read as “the student failed”. Unknown outcomes stay unknown, so the system cannot invent a struggling student out of its own backlog.

Know when you don't know

The profile carries an explicit readiness verdict: is there enough evidence to act on at all? A new student is treated as unknown, not as average.

Guided solve

The profile's most visible output: deciding when to make a student do it.

A decision, not a setting

A student cannot reasonably be asked whether they are ready to be pushed. That judgement needs the difficulty of the problem, their current state, how well the tutor knows them yet, and whether they are already distressed. It is made per problem, and it is conservative: every answer that amounts to “we don't know yet” resolves to showing the solution. Pushing a student who is not ready is worse than solving one they could have managed.

Earned praise

The tutor tracks how much the student actually wrote versus how much it revealed, and the closing words follow from that. A tutor that praises everyone stops being worth listening to.

The turn loop has no button

  1. Clear the wrong path

    Explain why it went wrong, keep what was right, then hand over from there.

  2. Prompt once

    In a register that follows the student's profile.

  3. Watch the canvas

    The student's handwriting ends the turn, not a tap.

  4. Treat silence and errors differently

    A wrong line gets a nudge that never reveals the answer. Silence gets encouragement, then the step.

Research we build on

None of these ideas are ours. Making them work on a live canvas is.

Flow

Csikszentmihalyi. Learning happens when challenge sits just above skill; boredom and anxiety sit either side.

Zone of proximal development

Vygotsky. The next problem should be reachable with a little help, not without any.

Productive failure and desirable difficulty

Kapur; Bjork. Struggle that ends in progress is the goal, not something to rescue students from.

The assistance dilemma

Koedinger and Aleven. Too much help and too little both hurt; the right amount depends on the learner.

Confusion in learning

D'Mello and Graesser. Confusion that resolves helps learning; confusion that does not becomes frustration.

Self-determination

Deci and Ryan. Autonomy and competence sustain motivation; scores and streaks crowd it out.
