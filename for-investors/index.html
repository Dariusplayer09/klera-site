The problem with AI tutors

They answer questions. Good teaching is not answering questions.

They are blind between messages

A chatbot sees a student twice: when the question is typed and when the next one is. Everything a real tutor watches for, the long stare at a blank page, the pressing harder, the rubbing out, happens in the gap.

They hand over the answer

The fastest way to satisfy a student is to solve it for them, and that is exactly what does not teach them. Products optimised for a satisfying reply are optimised against learning.

They live in the wrong place

Maths is done by hand. Making a student photograph their page, or retype an equation into a text box, breaks the work in half and loses everything about how they wrote it.

Why we are hard to copy

Three things, and each one is a different kind of difficulty.

One. We have data nobody else can collect

Pressure, speed, smoothness, the pause before a stroke, bursts of rubbing out. This only exists on a native tablet canvas with a pressure-sensitive pen. A web or chat competitor cannot get it by trying harder, or by buying it. They would have to become a different kind of product first.

And it is not one clever measurement. Klera runs six families of signal at once and combines them: how the pen moves, how the student asks for help, what their working actually contains, what we silently marked while they were not looking, what they said out loud, and which difficulty levels they have genuinely cleared. Thousands of raw measurements a session, folded into one model of one person.

That combination is the point. We have run this on real devices with real students, and the two we have measured are nothing alike: for one, distress shows up as pressing harder; for the other, pressure is completely flat and distress shows up as going still before the pen touches the page, eleven times further from their normal than anything they do when they are fine. A single industry-standard threshold would have caught one and been blind to the other. Because we measure six things and calibrate each one to the individual, we catch both. The measurements are published in full on the engineering page, not summarised.

Two. It teaches like a person, not like a page of text

Klera writes its maths as actual pen strokes on the student's own canvas, at the right size, in the right place, at reading speed, and talks while it does it. This took us four generations of engine to get right and it is the difference between a tutor sitting at your desk and a website open beside it. It is also an enormous amount of unglamorous engineering, which is its own kind of moat.

More importantly, it knows when not to answer. When the read says a student is ready, Klera writes one step and then stops and makes them produce the next line themselves, watching the page until they do, nudging without ever revealing the answer. That is the single hardest thing to get right in tutoring and the thing every other AI product gets wrong, because handing over the answer is what makes a user feel served in the moment and what stops them learning.

Three. It gets better the longer a student stays, and it checks its own work

The model of a student is personal and it takes weeks of real work to build. A student two months in is getting help calibrated to them specifically. Starting again somewhere else means starting from a blank sheet, which is a real reason to stay that has nothing to do with a contract.

It also grades itself. Every time Klera chooses how to help, it scores what the student did next and feeds that back in, so an approach that does not work for this particular student stops being used on them. Most tutoring products never close that loop: they pick a style, deliver it forever, and never find out.

The interesting column is the middle one. We are not trying to beat chat AI on answering questions; that fight is over and it is a commodity. We are trying to take the part of a private tutor that costs the most and cannot scale, which is a person watching you work, and make it cost nothing.

The business model

A consumer subscription first, because students and parents already pay for tutoring. Schools second, because they buy slowly and in bulk and they are a much better business once you are in.

Free

€0

The whole tutor, with a daily cap on how much help it will give. Enough to do your homework with it, enough to feel the difference, not enough to revise a whole syllabus on.

Klera Premium

around €8 to €12 a month

Uncapped help, exam simulation from your own past papers, and the learner profile carried across devices. Priced against a single hour of tutoring a month, which is what it replaces several of. A private tutor in most European markets is €30 to €60 an hour.

Schools

per seat, per year

Classes, assigned papers, automatic marking, and per-question analytics showing a teacher which topics a class actually lost marks on. Sold per department, renewed annually.

Prices are our current plan, not a live price list. Nothing is purchasable yet and the app is free during early access. We will set the real numbers when we choose the first market.

Why these three, in this order

Consumer first gives us the thing we most need, which is a lot of students using it on real work so the model of a student gets sharper. That usage is also the demo: a school does not buy an idea, it buys something its students are already asking for.

The school tier is where the money is, and it arrives on the back of the consumer one. The per-question marking analytics are genuinely painful work that a department currently does by hand, so the value is easy to state and easy to price.

Who this is for

The student

Fifteen to eighteen, working toward exams that decide where they go next, already doing maths on an iPad because that is what school gave them.

The parent

Already paying for tutoring, or wanting to and finding it costs more per hour than they can justify weekly. They are the one who pays for Premium.

The department

A head of maths with thirty students and no way to see which topics a class is quietly losing marks on until the mock results come back.

We are starting with maths, because it is the subject where working by hand matters most and where marks are the most objective. The engine is not maths-specific: anything a student works out on a page is reachable from here. The first market is being chosen now, and the curriculum layer is built to be swapped rather than rewritten.
