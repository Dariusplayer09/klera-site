The pen says more than the answer.

An Apple Pencil reports far more than position. Klera records four things per attempt, and none of them is what you wrote.

Force

How hard the pen presses into the glass, averaged across the attempt. For one of our two measured students, this was the entire signal.

Speed

How fast the nib actually travels while writing.

Jerkiness

Variation in speed within a stroke. The physical shape of a hesitant line.

Pause

The dead time before a stroke begins: thinking, or stalling. For the other student, this was the signal, and the clearest separation in all of our data.

None of this is content. The profile stores how you wrote, never what you wrote.

Measured against you, not everyone.

A fast writer is not a confident writer, and a heavy hand is not a stressed one. Raw numbers mean nothing across people, so Klera never compares a student to a population.

  1. Every stroke is read

    Force, speed, jerkiness and pause, for each attempt.

  2. Only calm work counts

    Attempts with erase bursts, help taps or frustrated language are refused, so a bad day never becomes your normal.

  3. Your baseline opens

    After twelve clean samples, and not one attempt sooner.

  4. Drift becomes visible

    New work is measured as distance from your own normal.

Before the twelfth sample the system reports nothing. Not zero: nothing. A number built from four samples is noise with a decimal point.

One of us presses harder when stuck. The other goes completely still.

We measured ourselves on separate iPads with separate baselines. Easy problems calmly until the baseline opened, then problems we genuinely could not do.

Student A presses harder

Pressure rose to more than double the top of his calm range while stuck. His handwriting stayed perfectly smooth; jerkiness read slightly below his normal.

Student B goes still

Pressure never moved. What moved was the pause before starting a stroke, far outside anything in his calm range, on the hardest problem of the session.

Neither signal detects the other person. One fixed threshold, the approach most systems ship, would have missed one of us, and there were only two of us.

What this does not prove

Two students and four genuine struggle attempts. That is enough to disprove “one signal fits everyone”, since one counterexample does that. It is nowhere near enough to set a threshold, and we have not moved one.

One measurement cuts against us. Student B produced a calm, solved, unaided attempt reading 0.57 on dysfluency, and a genuinely stuck one reading 0.56. For that person, fast confident writing and stuck writing look the same on that channel, so lowering its threshold would fire on confident work. That idea is recorded as dead.

While preparing this page we also found the detector that best separated student B could not fire at all, because a lookup upstream starved it of data. It is fixed, and the next measurement session is its first real test.

Help can be added, never taken away.

When the evidence says a student is in difficulty, the tutor moves to its most generous level of help. The rule only runs one way: nothing inferred is ever allowed to withdraw support.

It applies everywhere. Too small a sample returns nothing rather than a confident zero. No claim about a weakness is made before the evidence exists, because a tutor that believes a student has a misconception they do not have is worse than one that knows nothing.
