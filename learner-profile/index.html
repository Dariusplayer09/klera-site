01 — The premise

There is no such thing as what stuck looks like.

Every system that claims to detect struggle picks a signal — writing slows down, the pen gets shaky, the erasing starts — and sets a threshold on it. That works until you measure two actual people.

We did. The results are on this page, including the ones that were inconvenient.

02 — What it watches

The pen says more than the answer does.

An Apple Pencil reports far more than position. Every stroke carries force, speed, and the shape of its own acceleration; between strokes there are pauses. Klera records four things per attempt, and none of them is what you wrote.

Force

How hard the pen is pressed into the glass, averaged across the attempt.

Speed

How fast the nib actually travels while writing.

Jerkiness

Variation in speed within a stroke — the physical signature of a hesitant line.

Pause

The dead time before a stroke begins. Thinking, or stalling.

None of this is content. The profile never stores what a student wrote, only how they wrote it.

03 — The mechanism

Measured against you, not against everyone.

A fast writer is not a confident writer. A heavy hand is not a stressed hand. Raw numbers mean nothing across people, so Klera never compares a student to a population.

Instead it spends the first part of a session building a baseline of that student's own calm work — and only calm work. An attempt that involved erase bursts, help taps or frustrated language is refused entry to the baseline, so "normal" never quietly absorbs a bad day. Once twelve clean samples exist, and not one attempt before, deviation from that personal normal starts being meaningful.

04 — The finding

Two students. Two completely different tells.

We ran the measurement on ourselves, on separate iPads, with separate baselines. Each of us did easy problems calmly until the baseline opened, then genuinely fought problems we could not do — no acting.

One of us struggles by pressing harder. The other struggles by going still.

Neither signal detects the other person. The pressure reading that catches the first student is flat for the second. The pause that gives the second away never moves for the first. A single fixed threshold — the thing every competing approach ships — would have been blind to one of us, and we only had two people.

Student A

Presses harder.

Pressure strain rose to more than double the top of his calm range while stuck. His handwriting stayed perfectly smooth throughout — the jerkiness signal read slightly below his own normal.

Student B

Goes still.

Pressure never moved at all. What moved was the pause before starting a stroke — far outside anything in his calm band, on the hardest problem of the session.

What this does not prove

Two students. Four genuine struggle attempts between them. That is enough to falsify "one signal fits everyone" — a single counterexample does that — and nowhere near enough to set a threshold on. We have not moved one.

One measurement actively cuts against us: student B produced a calm, solved, unaided attempt reading 0.57 on dysfluency, and a genuinely stuck attempt reading 0.56. Fast confident writing and stuck writing are indistinguishable on that channel for that person. Any proposal to lower the dysfluency threshold toward those values would fire on confident work — so that proposal is dead, and it is recorded as dead.

We also found, while writing this, that the detector which best separated student B was structurally unable to fire: one stale lookup upstream starved it of the data it needed. It is fixed and unverified. The next measurement session is its first real test.

06 — What it changes

Support that can only be added, never taken away.

When the evidence says a student is in difficulty, the tutor moves to its most generous level of help, and the read of their state shifts toward confusion rather than flow. The rule is deliberately one-directional: physical distress may add support, but nothing inferred is ever allowed to withdraw it.

It is an asymmetry we apply everywhere. When a sample is too small to judge, it returns nothing rather than a confident zero. When the system has not seen enough of a student to name a weakness, it says nothing about their weaknesses. An invented claim about a student reaches the tutor as fact, and a tutor that believes a student has a misconception they do not have is worse than one that knows nothing.
